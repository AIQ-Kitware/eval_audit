\section{Results}
\label{sec:cases}

The results are three corpus-wide measurements, an instrument control, three case
studies, and one extension. The first measurement asks how much of the corpus can
be attempted at all (\S\ref{sec:census}); the second, what it costs to decide that
two runs are the same run (\S\ref{sec:coverage}). A control with a known answer
then establishes that our comparison layer can tell a reproduction from a
deliberately broken one (\S\ref{sec:phi2}). The third measurement is the broadest
reproduction set we
have, 703 paired Qwen comparisons, which establishes the base rate the case
studies are exceptions to (\S\ref{sec:qwen}). The case studies then instantiate
the two regimes of \S\ref{sec:model}. OLMo is
the \emph{partially recoverable} modern case: several disagreements attribute to
named unrecorded parameters (\S\ref{sec:olmo}). RedPajama-3B demonstrates
end-to-end execution under \emph{declared proxy} historical instruments
(\S\ref{sec:redpajama}). The original-paper classic models are the
\emph{non-identifiable} case: their producing instrument cannot be recovered from
surviving evidence (\S\ref{sec:classic}). The extension asks what happens when the
scorer itself is the unavailable component, and substitutes an open judge for a
withdrawn closed one (\S\ref{sec:openjudge}).

\subsection{What survives: a typed exclusion census}
\label{sec:census}

\S\ref{sec:intro} asks first how much of a historical corpus can be attempted at
all. A Stage-1 pass over the public HELM mirror answers it for this corpus, and
the answer is dominated by eligibility rather than by difficulty. Of $34{,}512$
discovered run records, $1{,}109$ ($3.2\%$) pass every gate of
\S\ref{sec:preconditions}; the surviving set covers $33$ of $366$ distinct models
and $61$ of $185$ distinct benchmarks. The exclusion reasons are not exclusive ---
a run typically fails several --- and their sizes are informative in themselves:
no local deployment for the model ($29{,}997$ runs), non-open weights ($18{,}781$),
excluded modality tags ($17{,}526$), size above the local ceiling ($7{,}624$),
non-text modality ($6{,}024$), missing model metadata ($2{,}894$), a closed judge
($484$), a gated dataset ($74$), and structural incompleteness ($14$). Whole
tracks vanish entirely: the vision, image-generation, and audio tracks contribute
$9{,}873$, $1{,}992$, and $1{,}737$ records and zero eligible runs.

Read as a fact about the record rather than about us, this says that a public
benchmark corpus decays overwhelmingly through \emph{access} --- weights,
deployment, modality, scale --- and only marginally through the damaged-record
failures that reproduction studies usually worry about: structural incompleteness
accounts for $14$ runs in $34{,}512$. It also bounds every reproducibility claim
in this paper: they are claims about the $3.2\%$, and the taxonomy's insistence
that a typed exclusion is an outcome rather than a discard is what makes that
bound visible instead of implicit.

\paragraph{Scope and freshness.} This census is read from a Stage-1 inventory
generated 2026-06-17 and not regenerated from a checked-in manifest, so it is
evidence about the corpus as crawled on that date rather than a reproducible
figure. It is also the most-cited and least-refreshed artifact in the project: a
separate estimate of the pre-v0.5 fraction of the corpus, carried in our own
working notes at ${\approx}59\%$, is contradicted by this inventory's $24.3\%$ and
should not be trusted until both are rebuilt. We therefore report the census as a
map and make no population-level prevalence claim on it.

Under a modern harness, byte-identical recipe agreement is unattainable. The

\subsection{Does the instrument work? A control with a known answer}
\label{sec:phi2}

Every number that follows is produced by our own comparison layer, so before
reading any of them it is worth asking whether that layer distinguishes a
reproduction from a non-reproduction at all. We ran one public row
(\texttt{mmlu:subject=philosophy}, \texttt{microsoft/phi-2}) under several
deployments plus a deliberately broken control. At temperature zero the instance
agreement against the public row is $0.990$--$0.997$ whether the model is served
through vLLM, through the same vLLM inside the pinned container, or through
Hugging Face. The control --- the identical recipe at temperature $1$, which
\emph{should} fail --- lands at $0.778$, in the lowest agreement bucket.

The control is the point. A comparison layer that reported high agreement for the
temperature-1 arm would be measuring nothing, and the spread across serving paths
at temperature zero ($0.007$) is small beside the gap to the broken arm ($0.212$),
so the instrument separates the variable it is meant to detect from the ones it is
meant to tolerate.

\paragraph{A repeatability floor, and what it costs to ignore it.} That $0.007$
spread invites a causal reading --- that the serving path costs agreement, and the
container costs a little more --- and the runs behind it refute that reading. Five
executions of this row exist at temperature zero under the same recipe and the
same deployment. \emph{Four are byte-identical to one another across all $311$
instances, and one of the four is the containerized run.} The fifth differs from
all of them on $4$ instances, each a single-token multiple-choice answer that
flips to a different letter.

So containerization changed nothing measurable here, and the apparent ordering
between the arms is an artifact of \emph{which} execution each report happened to
pair against: the arm we would have called best is the lone outlier, and the arm
we would have called slightly degraded sits in the consensus. What the five runs
do measure is a repeatability floor --- $4$ of $311$ instances, about $1.3\%$,
moving between executions that differ in nothing we recorded. No disagreement
smaller than that floor should be attributed to anything, and we have no such
floor for the corpora in the rest of this section (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

These runs pair against no official row --- they compare a local execution to a
public one under conditions we chose --- so they are reported here as instrument
validation and are excluded from every corpus count in this section.

\subsection{Qwen: the base rate on a modern harness}
\label{sec:qwen}

The Qwen family gives the broadest reproduction set in this study, and therefore
the base rate against which the case studies read as exceptions. Eight
models --- \texttt{qwen1.5-7b}, \texttt{-14b}, \texttt{-32b}, \texttt{-72b},
\texttt{qwen1.5-110b-chat}, \texttt{qwen2-72b-instruct},
and \texttt{qwen2.5-7b/72b-instruct-turbo} --- were replayed from spec and paired
against their official rows, yielding $703$ built comparisons from $775$ planned
run entries with no skipped entries.

Instance-level agreement with the official is measured at zero tolerance, so a
run counts as agreeing only where the per-instance metric values match exactly.
Across the $703$ comparisons the median is $0.990$ and the mean $0.975$;
$608$ ($86\%$) sit at or above $0.95$, $159$ agree exactly, and $10$ fall
below $0.80$, with a minimum of $0.66$. Per-model medians span $0.973$
(\texttt{qwen2.5-7b-instruct-turbo}) to $1.000$ (\texttt{qwen1.5-110b-chat}).

On a modern harness with a from-spec replay, then, most historical Qwen rows
reproduce at or near the instance level: the median run agrees with the official
on $99\%$ of its instances at zero tolerance. Reproduction failure is not the
common case here; it is the tail. That is what makes the rest of the section
interpretable. A study that reported only the disagreements would imply that
historical benchmark reproduction is generally broken, and this corpus says
otherwise. What the tail costs to explain, rather than how large it is, is the
subject of \S\ref{sec:olmo} onward.

\paragraph{Scope and freshness.} The counts and agreement values are read from the
store's derived reproducibility rows; all $1406$ run paths recorded in the
comparison manifests resolve on disk --- exactly two per comparison, one official
and one local, so no Qwen figure depends on choosing among repeated attempts
(\S\ref{sec:olmo}) --- and the set is re-renderable and hashable without
re-execution. These are \emph{reproductions} of rows HELM already
published. The separate arm promised as new Qwen 3.5 evaluations is not reported
here: $72$ runs of \texttt{qwen3.5-9b-base} covering \texttt{mmlu} ($57$),
\texttt{legalbench} and \texttt{wmt\_14} ($5$ each), and one run each of
\texttt{boolq}, \texttt{commonsense}, \texttt{gsm}, \texttt{med\_qa} and
\texttt{narrative\_qa} exist as raw artifacts with per-instance statistics, but
they have never been passed through the analysis and reporting stages, and no
store exists for them. A further nine run directories --- seven \texttt{math} and
two \texttt{natural\_qa} --- were created but are empty, so those two scenarios
were attempted and produced nothing. Because HELM published no Qwen 3.5 rows there is no official
side to pair against, so that arm needs a reporting path of its own rather than
the agreement machinery used here.

One result from those artifacts is worth stating now, because it is a scoring
hazard rather than a score. Reading the stored completions directly, the base
model emits \texttt{<think>} reasoning tags on a plain completions prompt --- on
$322$ of $470$ \texttt{narrative\_qa} instances ($68.5\%$) and $502$ of $1000$ on
\texttt{boolq} ($50.2\%$), and on \emph{zero} of the $24{,}416$ instances across
\texttt{mmlu}, \texttt{wmt\_14}, \texttt{med\_qa}, \texttt{legalbench},
\texttt{gsm}, and \texttt{commonsense}. The split is exactly free-form generation
against constrained answer shapes, with nothing in between, and it does not track
prompt content: the \texttt{boolq} perturbation arms leak at the same rate as
canonical. With \texttt{max\_tokens=5} a \texttt{<think>} prefix destroys a
\texttt{boolq} answer outright, so those two cells would be upper bounds depressed
by a measured formatting failure rather than capability measurements. Whatever
scores this arm eventually reports, they have to carry the leakage rate beside
them --- and since an instruct model of the same family would not do this, the
rate is a measurable property of the base/instruct boundary rather than a defect
to be fixed.

\subsection{OLMo: attributing modern disagreements to unrecorded parameters}
\label{sec:olmo}

We study six OLMo-family models
\citep{groeneveld2024olmo,olmo2_2024,muennighoff2024olmoe} --- four recent instruct models
(\texttt{olmoe-1b-7b-0125-instruct}, \texttt{olmo-2-1124-7b/13b-instruct},
\texttt{olmo-2-0325-32b-instruct}) on capability benchmarks
(\texttt{bbq}, \texttt{gpqa}, \texttt{ifeval} \citep{zhou2023ifeval},
\texttt{mmlu\_pro} \citep{hendrycks2021mmlu}) and two older base
models with broad classic-track coverage. Three attributions and one open puzzle
emerge.

\paragraph{(a) A tokenizer special-token append (confirmed).} During from-spec
reproduction of \texttt{narrative\_qa}, local OLMo-7B emitted the same
prompt-independent boilerplate for \emph{every} instance while the official
conditioned correctly, with \texttt{prompts\_equal=True} at the string level. The
first hypothesis --- fp16 numerical instability --- was \emph{refuted} by a
deployment sweep. The cause was a tokenizer issue: \texttt{OLMo-7B-hf}'s tokenizer
appends an \texttt{<|endoftext|>} token to the prompt under vLLM's default
\texttt{add\_special\_tokens=True}, which the original deployment did not do and
which HELM never recorded. Serving the append-free tokenizer and forwarding
\texttt{add\_special\_tokens=false} restores prompt conditioning. This is the
paradigm case: an apparent flat irreproducibility was a single unrecorded
request-time flag. The coordinate that differs is the deployment --- the prompt
tokens themselves (Table~\ref{tab:identifiability}, row ``tokenizer
special-token behavior'') --- while the engine default that supplied it sits in
the instrument (\S\ref{sec:model}); fully recoverable either way.

\paragraph{(b) A chat-template version dependency (confirmed).} HELM always calls
\texttt{apply\_chat\_template(\ldots, add\_generation\_prompt=True)}, but that flag
is a no-op unless the chat template \emph{implements} it. The OLMoE template
shipped with HELM's older \texttt{transformers} ignored the flag --- so the model
was fed the prompt \emph{without} the trailing assistant-turn marker --- whereas a
modern \texttt{transformers}/vLLM ships an updated template that honors it and
\emph{appends} the marker, changing the prompt and the output. Rendering with
\texttt{add\_generation\_prompt=false} on the modern stack moves the output back
toward the official, confirmed empirically. The attribution is to the
\emph{tokenizer/chat-template version} coordinate, whose effective behavior
depends on the (unrecorded) \texttt{transformers} version --- a clean example of a
software-version dependency hiding inside a recipe.

\paragraph{(c) Load precision: a discovery, with a deductive leg (partially
corroborated).} HELM's \texttt{HuggingFaceClient} loads a model with only the kwargs
the deployment config supplies; for the OLMo instruct deployments that is
\texttt{device\_map=auto} and \emph{no} \texttt{torch\_dtype}. Under every
\texttt{transformers} 4.x, an unpinned load defaults to \texttt{float32}, ignoring the
checkpoint's bf16 config (reading the checkpoint dtype is a v5 change). Since the
OLMoE architecture landed in \texttt{transformers}${\sim}$4.45, a January-2025 run
sits squarely in the fp32-default regime. This is a \emph{deductive} argument ---
read off the loader source and the version-dependent default, not fit to outputs
--- and is the stronger leg; its residual is the one parameter it depends on, the
exact \texttt{transformers} version, which the run directory bounds but does not
record.

A separate, small \emph{discovery probe} corroborates it: an oracle-scored
deployment-match sweep over $n\approx 12$ \texttt{ifeval} instances finds float32
candidate cells with the highest agreement to the sampled public completions
(Table~\ref{tab:fp32}). The winning \emph{engine} depends on architecture:
OLMoE is MoE, so vLLM's fused-kernel float32 path is infeasible on workstation
cards and only in-process \texttt{transformers} float32 reproduces the sampled
OLMoE outputs, whereas the dense OLMo-2 samples matched most closely under vLLM
float32.

The prediction this licensed was that float32 alone would close the gap, and it
did not. Two ordinary-path \texttt{ifeval} runs at float32 --- on
\texttt{olmo-2-1124-7b-instruct} and \texttt{-13b-instruct}, through the normal
HELM path rather than the probe, at the full $1082$ per-instance rows per side ---
move the strict accuracy from $0.7911$ to $0.7597$ against an official $0.6929$ on
the 7B, and from $0.8555$ to $0.8121$ against $0.7298$ on the 13B. Both close
about a third of the drift: directionally right, magnitude wrong. The same
fraction and the same sign on two model sizes make the residual systematic rather
than noise, which is what turns a failed prediction into the next measurement.

\paragraph{(d) The residual is the serving engine.} Serving the same float32
weights through Hugging Face \texttt{transformers} rather than vLLM reproduces the
official completions \emph{byte for byte}: on the 7B at the full $n=541$
\texttt{ifeval} instances, $539$ match exactly ($99.63\%$), identically under both
\texttt{device\_map} settings. Rendering the same cell with the modern chat
template instead of the old one collapses it to $13/541$, which independently
re-confirms (b) at full scale. Decode semantics are not involved: true greedy and
HELM's \texttt{do\_sample=True, temperature=1e-7} produce byte-identical
completions on every probe instance. Table~\ref{tab:closure} states the
decomposition.

Same weights, same precision, same prompt tokens, same greedy decode --- only
vLLM in place of Hugging Face --- moves a published benchmark metric by
$+0.067$ on the 7B and $+0.082$ on the 13B. That is the paper's sharpest single

provenance standard of \S\ref{sec:provenance} deliberately does \emph{not} cover.

The two non-matching instances are worth their own sentence, because they are the
thesis in miniature. Both are long-form generations that agree for over a hundred
characters and then diverge at a single near-tie token --- ``his partner Calvert
Vaux'' against ``his associate Calvert Vaux'' at character $153$, and an inserted
adjective at character $189$ --- after which the completions never re-converge. A
recovered configuration does not eliminate near-tie sensitivity; it reduces how
often a near-tie is reached.

What this is still short of: one benchmark, one model family, two sizes,
instruct-only, and completion-level evidence rather than a metric-level run
through the ordinary HELM path on held-out instances. We therefore report the
substrate as \emph{recovered} on this cell and keep (c)'s general claim at
discovery grade (\S\ref{sec:olmo-confirm}).

deployment sweep ranks float32 at a perfect match ($1.000$), float16 below it

predicted ordering, on a family that played no part in forming the hypothesis.

\paragraph{(e) A divergence that was our own instrument.} Table~\ref{tab:fp32}
records an inconsistency we could not initially explain: the in-process
\texttt{transformers} float32 probe matched the sampled OLMoE outputs exactly but
gave near-zero quasi-match for the dense OLMo-2 models, even though those
officials are \emph{recorded as} \texttt{HuggingFaceClient} deployments.
Tokenization was byte-identical across paths, so it was not a prompt difference,
and a first-token disagreement under identical prompt tokens localized it to the
forward pass. For one draft of this paper we reported it as an unresolved
systematic divergence, listing a defect in our own probe among the candidate
causes.

It was that defect. The probe passed its \texttt{add\_generation\_prompt} and
\texttt{add\_special\_tokens} arguments as \texttt{0}/\texttt{1} where the client
expected \texttt{true}/\texttt{false}, so the dense sweeps were scoring a
configuration they had not actually requested; the corrected sweep ranks the
same-named cell at a full match. We report this rather than quietly deleting it
because it is the failure mode \S\ref{sec:limitations} warns about --- the audit
tool generating a disagreement of its own --- caught here only because the
inconsistency was followed instead of interpreted. An execution-stack divergence
and a bug in the instrument that measures execution stacks present identically at
the point of observation.

\paragraph{When a store holds more than one attempt.} The base-model
\texttt{olmo-7b} rows illustrate a bookkeeping hazard that is really a
reproducibility one. Each of them has \emph{two} local runs against the same
deployment: one produced by the collapsed configuration of (a), scoring $0.000$
with the prompt-independent boilerplate, and one produced after the tokenizer fix,
scoring in line with the official. Both are legitimate records of what we
executed, and the store keeps both. An aggregate that averages over local attempts
therefore reports MMLU as $0.295/0.144$ --- half the true value --- while one that
selects a single attempt per row reports $0.295/0.287$. The selecting version is
what is on disk and what we would cite.

The scope of the exposure is worth stating precisely, because it is narrow: $71$
of the $149$ OLMo packets carry two attempts and \emph{all $71$ are
\texttt{olmo-7b}}. Every other model in the OLMo corpus, including all four
instruct \texttt{ifeval} packets whose drift is quoted above and in
\S\ref{sec:gptoss}, pairs one official row to one local run, as do all $703$ Qwen
packets, all four \texttt{gpt-oss-20b} packets, and both packets in each
RedPajama era. No number in this section other than the base-model
\texttt{olmo-7b} aggregates depends on a selection rule.

The general point is that a comparison layer facing multiple attempts must
\emph{choose}, and the choice is a judgment about which execution the number
describes rather than a detail of the join. We halved this aggregate once by not
choosing, and re-derived the halved figure a second time while preparing this
paper by averaging the per-attempt reports directly. Both times the artifacts were
correct and the reduction over them was not, which is the same failure mode as
citing a benchmark score without identifying the run that produced it, one level
up. We cite no base-model OLMo aggregate until the inputs behind it are hashed to
the attempts they select.

\subsubsection{Generalization probe: GPT-OSS-20B (candidate)}
\label{sec:gptoss}
As a test that the machinery generalizes beyond OLMo, we replayed the four
ungated-judge public \texttt{gpt-oss-20b} rows (\texttt{bbq}, \texttt{gpqa},
\texttt{ifeval}, \texttt{mmlu\_pro}) from spec, after diagnosing and fixing a
null-content chat crash (a null-vs-empty-string serving difference between the
hosted and local engines; normalizing null$\to$``'' is faithful). The regenerated
report shows local and public headline scores agreeing closely: three of the four
scenarios agree exactly on every core metric, and the fourth (\texttt{bbq})
differs by $0.004$, for a worst-case squared error of $1.6{\times}10^{-5}$ ---

\emph{promising candidate}, not a result: the report layer is current-code and the
run artifacts survive, but they have not been hashed against the stored report, so
run-artifact and comparison provenance must be established (re-render, preserve,
hash, and pass a manifest-based acceptance audit) before the numbers are cited.

\subsection{RedPajama-3B: execution under declared proxy-era instruments}
\label{sec:redpajama}

Not every historical run is unrecoverable. \texttt{redpajama-incite-base-3b-v1}
\citep{together2023redpajama} was run end-to-end through \emph{both} classic-era instruments (v0.2.4 and v0.3.0
scoring containers, \S\ref{sec:eras}) on a small workstation, exercising a
validation ladder: image sanity, instrument fidelity on a \texttt{pandas}-sensitive
scenario (byte-for-byte instance identity, no model), an end-to-end single run, a
full per-era packet, and a per-scenario data-availability audit (data-fetch/auth
failures are classified as environment SKIPs, not instrument failures). This
demonstrates \emph{procedural reproduction under a declared proxy instrument}
(\S\ref{sec:targets}): the era container reproduces \emph{a} faithful scoring
environment for the classic recipe. We are careful to distinguish this from
reconstructing the \emph{original} producing instrument --- the two later eras
carry byte-identical public artifacts for the shared runs (carried forward, not
re-run), so ``two eras agree'' is one public number measured by two instruments,
not two independent confirmations.

Both eras planned and built two packets each, with no skipped run entries, and the
outcome splits by scenario. On \texttt{mmlu} the replay is exact: all eight core
metrics --- exact, quasi-exact, prefix, and quasi-prefix match, on both the test
and valid splits --- agree with the official at $\Delta = 0$, identically under
v0.2.4 and v0.3.0. On \texttt{synthetic\_reasoning\_natural} they do not agree,
and not in the direction drift would predict: the official reports exactly
$0.0000$ on all six set-match metrics while the local run scores $0.147$ to
$0.184$, again identically under both eras. A published zero across every metric
of a scenario is not a value a working scorer ordinarily produces, so we treat
this as an open question about the official record rather than as a reproduction
failure, and count it as neither pending diagnosis. It is a concrete instance of
why the record must be screened before a disagreement is attributed
(\S\ref{sec:preconditions}).

\subsection{GPT-J, GPT-NeoX, OPT: origin-instrument non-identifiability}
\label{sec:classic}

The original-paper classic models
\citep{wang2021gptj,black2022gptneox,zhang2022opt} are the negative counterpart.
Era-replaying them
fails the shim's class preflight: their \texttt{run\_spec.json} names
\texttt{helm.benchmark.basic\_metrics.BasicMetric}, a class path that resolves in
\emph{no} released HELM version. An investigation over a blob-less clone of the
HELM history establishes that the stored path is a once-migrated hybrid --- a bulk
\texttt{benchmark.}$\to$\texttt{helm.benchmark.} prefix rewrite (applied at the
\texttt{src/helm/} rename) of a run-spec whose metric class was still \emph{flat} at
production time. Reversing the naive migration triangulates the producing code to
an ${\sim}$4-week window of \emph{unreleased pre-v0.1.0} HELM (after scenarios
were nested but before metrics were). The class-path \emph{shape} (flat vs.\
subpackage) is thus a sharper origin fingerprint than release dates.

The producing instrument for GPT-J, GPT-NeoX, and OPT is therefore
\emph{non-identifiable from the surviving public and repository evidence we
examined}: no released version resolves the recorded class, and the triangulated
origin is unreleased. This is category (5) of \S\ref{sec:taxonomy}, not a
repeatability failure. Because
building the true origin instrument is infeasible, we treat any later-era run as a
\emph{declared proxy} and make the substitution explicit: a self-verifying
class-path canonicalization remaps the migrated class only when its known
relocation resolves to the same leaf (a genuinely wrong era pin still fails
loudly). We scope the claim carefully --- non-identifiable \emph{relative to
surviving evidence}, not an absolute impossibility statement about hypothetical
private provider archives.

\subsection{Substituting an open judge for a withdrawn one}
\label{sec:openjudge}

The preconditions of \S\ref{sec:preconditions} treat an unavailable judge as
blocking procedural reproduction while leaving the released verdicts auditable.
That is the conservative reading. The stronger question is whether a judge that
can still be served substitutes for one that cannot, and if so under what
criterion --- a declared substitution, made explicit exactly as a proxy era is.

Before substituting anything we establish that the scoring step is reproducible at
all when nothing is substituted. Reattaching the \emph{original} judge annotations
to a reconstructed scenario state and re-running the real official metric returns
the published statistics on all six model-graded benchmarks available to us
(\texttt{xstest}, \texttt{wildbench}, \texttt{harm\_bench},
\texttt{simple\_safety\_tests}, \texttt{anthropic\_red\_team},
\texttt{omni\_math}): $12{,}750$ instance rows and $69$ aggregate rows, zero
failures, maximum absolute error $0$ on five benchmarks and $2{\times}10^{-14}$
on the sixth. This is artifact reconstruction in the sense of
\S\ref{sec:targets} --- no model executed, the judge's forward pass read as stored
data --- and it is the control the substitution results are measured against: any
disagreement below is attributable to the judge, because the surrounding
machinery reproduces exactly when the judge is held fixed.

We then ran two open judges, \texttt{qwen3.5-27b} and \texttt{qwen3.6-35b-a3b},
over the released responses of two model-graded scenarios, three replicates each,
and compared them against both official judges. The comparison that matters is not
open-versus-official on its own but open-versus-official measured against the
\emph{official-versus-official} baseline: two closed judges already disagree with
each other, and a substitute is admissible when it disagrees no more than they do.

The split in that table is by scenario, not by judge, and that is the result:
judge substitutability is a property of the metric rather than of the substitute.
On the label scenario both open judges agree with an official judge \emph{better
than the two official judges agree with each other} ($\kappa = 0.936$ and $0.928$
against a $0.829$ baseline), and are almost perfectly stable across replicates. On
the continuous scenario neither does. Both carry a systematic negative bias of
roughly $0.8$ points on the scoring scale, correlate less well with an official
judge than the officials do with one another, and change $39$--$43\%$ of instance
scores between replicates of the same judge on the same responses.

The practical consequence is that a withdrawn judge does not uniformly rule out
procedural reproduction. Where the metric is a label, a declared open-judge
substitution is defensible on this evidence and the run returns to scope. Where
it is a continuous score, it is not: the substitution introduces a bias larger
than the disagreement it is meant to stand in for, and the replicate instability
means a single substituted run is not even self-consistent. Reporting the
official-versus-official baseline is what makes that call decidable rather than
rhetorical, and it is the same move as declaring a proxy era instead of quietly
running a later one.

\paragraph{Scope and freshness.} Both analyses are keyed to a response-set hash,
so the open arms score exactly the responses the officials scored, and no model
was re-run to produce them. Two scenarios is a narrow base for a claim about
label versus continuous metrics generally, and the parse-failure rate on the
continuous scenario ($15.9\%$ for the $27$b arm, $2.0\%$ for the $35$b) is high
enough that some of the bias may be parser rather than judge.

The two arms reported here are a slice of a wider grid that has been executed but
not analysed: six judge sizes from $0.8$b to $35$b across five benchmarks,
$75$ attempt directories in all, three replicates each at temperature zero. Two
things are already visible in it and both cut against a simple reading. Parse
success tracks judge size cleanly --- it runs from $14\%$ at $0.8$b to $98\%$ at
$35$b on the continuous scenario --- while agreement with the official does not,
so the obvious health check does not predict the quantity of interest. And a
format-clean judge can be badly miscalibrated in a way no format check would
catch: on a near-single-class safety benchmark, the smallest arms parse almost
perfectly while scoring far from the official, which is a label inversion wearing
the appearance of a successful run. We hold the substitution claim to the two
analysed scenarios until the rest of the grid is scored, and note that agreement
on a $99\%$ one-class benchmark is close to a false-positive rate rather than a
substitutability measurement.
