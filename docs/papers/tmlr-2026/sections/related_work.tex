\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{HELM:} Our work concerns HELM, the Holistic Evaluation of Language Models framework of \citet{liang2023holistic}. HELM addressed the issue of a fragmented model evaluation landscape where each model was reported on its own largely non-overlapping set of benchmarks. Before HELM, a given model had on average been evaluated on only $17.9\%$ of the core HELM scenarios, with some prominent models ``not sharing a single scenario in common.'' Thus, cross-model comparisons were assembled from numbers produced under different prompts, decoding settings, and harnesses.

HELM reframed evaluation around three elements of holistic measurement. First, broad coverage: scenarios are organized by an explicit taxonomy of task, domain, and language, so that what the benchmark covers and leaves out is stated rather than assumed. Second, multi-metric measurement: accuracy, calibration, robustness, fairness, bias, toxicity, and efficiency are reported for every scenario, rather than collapsing a model to a single accuracy score. Third and most relevant to our paper, standardization of the conditions under which models are run: all models are evaluated through one adaptation procedure, emphasizing that ``our object of evaluation is the language model, not a scenario-specific system.''

Under this protocol HELM benchmarked 30 models across 42 scenarios, raising average per-model scenario coverage from $17.9\%$ to $96.0\%$. It did so through a modular, extensible evaluation framework that emits detailed artifacts, including the exact prompt issued to each model for each instance, the raw completion returned, the resolved run specification, and the per-instance and aggregate statistics computed from them.

HELM was designed not as a static snapshot but as ``a living benchmark for the community, continuously updated with new scenarios, metrics, and models.'' It has since grown into a large public database of evaluation results, spanning 366 distinct models and 215 scenarios. A standardized protocol, published records of the prompts and completions behind every number, and a large public archive make HELM ideal target for a reproducibility audit: enough is released to attempt a faithful replay, and enough time has passed that drift can be measured and attributed.

\textbf{Taxonomies of reproduction:} There have been many attempts to standardize the language and taxonomize reproducibility efforts. \citet{goodman2016does} introduces a trichotomy: \emph{methods} reproducibility, implementing the original experimental and computational procedures with the same data and tools to obtain the same results; \emph{results} reproducibility, obtaining corroborating results from an independent study matched to the original as closely as possible; and \emph{inferential} reproducibility, drawing the same conclusions from an independent replication or from a reanalysis of the original. Adjacent vocabularies cut the space differently rather than restating it. ACM artifact badging is a reviewing instrument, awarding \emph{Results Reproduced} or \emph{Results Replicated} according to whether a subsequent study used author-supplied artifacts or none \citep{acm2020badging}; this covers Goodman's first two categories and has no counterpart to the third. It is also less settled than it appears --- ACM interchanged those two terms in 2020 to adopt the NISO standard, so a badge name denotes opposite conditions on either side of that revision, which is itself a reason to state reproduction claims in terms of what was held fixed rather than by category name. \citet{gundersen2018state} measure something else again: grading 400 AI papers on how completely each documents its method, data, and experiment, they find roughly a quarter of the variables needed for reproduction were recorded at all. We adapt Goodman's trichotomy rather than replace it. Our claim-level robustness is inferential reproducibility, narrowed to whether a conclusion survives plausible execution substrates; our procedural reproduction is methods reproducibility with its central premise removed. Goodman's formulation requires ``the same data and tools,'' and in a historical benchmark corpus the tools are precisely what did not survive: HELM's harness is public and each run specification is frozen, but the serving stack, model revision, load precision, and tokenizer state behind a given completion were never recorded and cannot be reconstituted, so any replay necessarily runs on a declared-equivalent instrument rather than the original.

Two things follow that the trichotomy does not supply. First, because these corpora publish per-instance prompts, completions, and metric records, the published aggregates can be recomputed from the published outputs with no model execution at all. We make that a first-class target, artifact reconstruction (\S\ref{sec:targets}), and it is not merely a cheaper substitute for re-execution. For a model-graded scenario whose judge is no longer served, re-execution is impossible and artifact reconstruction is the only target available: the judge's per-instance verdicts survive in the record even though the judge itself does not, so a scorer or migration defect remains detectable with no judge in the loop. A trichotomy in which every category re-runs something has nowhere to put that case, and would discard a body of evidence that is still intact. Second, and more consequentially, the trichotomy names which kind of reproduction was attempted but not why one failed: it offers no way to attribute a disagreement to a particular unrecorded parameter. That attribution is the second axis of our taxonomy (\S\ref{sec:taxonomy}), and it is worth having because re-run results move under implementation and environment detail \citep{pineau2021checklist,dodge2019show}, methods reproducibility and results variance are separate properties \citep{bouthillier2019unreproducible}, and benchmark variance decomposes into separately identifiable sources \citep{bouthillier2021variance}.

\textbf{The execution substrate:} Two lines of work bear on how the execution of an evaluation shapes its score, and our contribution sits between them. The first concerns the recipe. Benchmark scores move under prompt formatting, few-shot ordering, and answer-selection method, sometimes enough to reorder leaderboards \citep{sclar2024quantifying,mizrahi2024state,alzahrani2024targets}; three implementations of MMLU give three different numbers and three different rankings \citep{hfleaderboard2023mmlu}; and maintainers of a widely used harness report from experience that evaluation results are rarely comparable across setups \citep{biderman2024lessons}. Machine translation confronted a version of this earlier and resolved it by pinning and reporting the scoring implementation itself \citep{post2018clarity}; our version-pinned ``era'' containers (\S\ref{sec:system}) generalize that discipline to a historical corpus whose scorer has since changed underneath it. We are otherwise orthogonal to this line. Rather than varying the recipe to demonstrate that scores are fragile, we hold it fixed by replaying the resolved specification verbatim --- which is precisely what isolates everything downstream of it.

What lies downstream is the execution substrate, and a second line of work establishes that each of its components moves outputs on its own. Greedy decoding through Hugging Face \texttt{transformers} \citep{wolf2020transformers} and through vLLM \citep{kwon2023vllm} differs in kernels, batching, tokenizer handling, and stop-sequence semantics; attention-kernel choice \citep{dao2022flashattention} and reduced-precision inference \citep{dettmers2022int8} perturb logits enough to flip near-ties, and quantization shifts benchmark scores by amounts that vary with task, language, and context length \citep{mekala2025doesquantizationaffectmodels,marchisio2024doesquantizationaffectmultilingual,shi2025systematiccharacterizationllmquantization,shen2024exploring}. Batching is itself a numerical variable \citep{yuan2026understanding}, and because common kernels are not batch-invariant, server-side batching makes a hosted endpoint nondeterministic even at temperature zero \citep{thinkingmachines2025nondeterminism,atil2024nondeterminism} --- which bears directly on the fraction of the open-weight corpus served by external providers, whose batching state is unrecorded and unrecoverable. Nor are the weights more stable than the stack that runs them: a model name resolves to different checkpoints over time, and behavior behind a fixed name can change measurably between releases \citep{chen2023chatgpt}.

Every one of these results is established by controlled experiment --- choose an intervention, run both arms, measure the difference --- and each therefore answers whether some factor \emph{can} move a score. Our setting inverts the inference. Only one arm survives, the substrate that produced it cannot be re-executed, and several unrecorded parameters are confounded at once, so the question is not whether precision or tokenizer state can move a score but which of them accounts for \emph{this} discrepancy, in a run whose instrument is gone. That is an identification problem rather than an ablation, and it can end in either of two ways, both of which this paper reports. Either the surviving record narrows the candidates far enough that a specific unrecorded parameter can be named as responsible, or it does not, and the record remains consistent with several substrates that would have produced the published number equally well. A controlled comparison never faces the second outcome, because it fixes the intervention in advance. We accordingly make the effects quantitative against published benchmark metrics, name the parameter behind a discrepancy where the surviving evidence supports it, and say so explicitly where it does not.

\textbf{Detection versus attribution:} The immediate predecessor of this work, \textit{Every Eval Ever} \citep{eee2026}, introduces a normalized per-instance representation that makes official-vs-local discrepancies \emph{visible} across heterogeneous harness versions, and reports detected gaps on Pythia-6.9B \citep{biderman2023pythia}, Vicuna-7B, and Falcon-7B. Its appendix explicitly leaves open whether a residual difference arises from checkpoint, quantization, precision, tokenizer, or generation-kernel choices. The present paper takes that open question as its subject: we supply the machinery to attribute and sometimes close such gaps, and characterize when the answer is underdetermined.

\textbf{Validity is a different axis:} A parallel critique asks whether a published benchmark number means what it claims: open corpora invite leakage, and a small model trained on a released HELM scenario can top the leaderboard on that scenario while collapsing on unseen ones \citep{pitfalls2025open}. Model-graded metrics add a further dependence on a judge whose own version and availability are rarely pinned \citep{zheng2023judge}. These threaten the \emph{validity} of a score; we ask the prior question of whether a score can be reconstructed at all, and treat unavailable judges as an eligibility filter on procedural reproduction rather than as a reproduction outcome, leaving such runs within scope for artifact reconstruction.

\textbf{Provenance and artifact preservation:} Our provenance recommendation (\S\ref{sec:provenance}) is in the spirit of model and dataset documentation \citep{mitchell2019modelcards,gebru2021datasheets} and of benchmark-quality standards that find most widely used benchmarks do not permit straightforward replication \citep{reuel2024betterbench}, but targets the \emph{execution} rather than the model, the data, or the benchmark design: what must be recorded so that a score is reconstructible. The closest operational analogue is containerized, digest-pinned execution \citep{boettiger2015docker}; we extend it with version-pinned scoring instruments for historical corpora and a per-parameter identifiability analysis, and show that three fields close most of the gap.
